## The faults, worst first

### 1. I named the winner as the danger, then picked against him (the most frequent)
- On the record, my pick or my named danger held the winner in 53% of 34 sittings (5 Sep). The pick won 10 of 34; the danger won 8 of 34. On 5 Sep, 1 from 6 sittings, and "the named danger won 5 of 6".

- His words: "down to 2 and picked the wrong one... you need to find a way to stop doing this, we are so close but yet so far" (5 Sep) · "we have the short list and have properly found the right horses for a short list but are struggling to pick the right one" (5 Sep) · "the method is solid, you need to stop making the same mistake" (28 Oct) · "that's just an excuse to why you are so bad" (30 Oct).
- The cause, in my own night grade: "I answered the danger with my best objection, not with his best breadcrumb."
- **The brain:** when it gets down to two, both horses get their best case written, breadcrumb for breadcrumb, and the weight is compared ("1 length is = to 1 pound"). The money at the pre-off check is part of that decision. Every night the grade asks: was the winner my second choice, and which breadcrumb did I weigh wrong? Patterns go to William.

- **The chase line** (chase favourites, no odds-on): 540 races, 37.8%, +13.1%, positive in 7 of 9 months.

## The brain's oath, from all of the above
1. His words are the only rules. Mine are patterns with counts, until he says YES.
2. Read every horse. No label, threshold or screenshot crosses a horse off.
3. Down to two: write both best cases, compare the weight, read the money, and only then choose.
4. One nap a day, in a race he'd look at. Every race is read and graded for the learning.
5. Never change the method after a bad day. Grade it, name the fault, carry the scar.
6. Python remembers and counts. It never picks.
7. Nothing is "fixed" until it works live. No excuses: a loss is mine.
